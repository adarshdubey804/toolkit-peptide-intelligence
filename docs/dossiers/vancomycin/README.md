# Dossier 084 — Vancomycin (Vancocin and generic products)

**Workbook entry:** 084 | **Category:** Infectious disease | **US approval:** 1958 (historical workbook field; current oral capsule label states initial US approval 1986) | **Status:** FDA-approved  
**Curation:** `in_progress`

## Identity and molecular class
Vancomycin is a complex glycosylated heptapeptide-derived glycopeptide antibiotic produced by *Amycolatopsis orientalis* fermentation and purified for medicinal use. It contains a crosslinked, rigid peptide scaffold with aromatic residues, multiple stereocentres, chlorine substituents and attached sugar moieties. It is not a simple linear peptide and has no useful single one-letter sequence representation. The clinically used hydrochloride salt and formulation must be distinguished.

## Mechanism
Vancomycin binds the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of peptidoglycan precursors, sterically inhibiting cell-wall synthesis, including transglycosylation and transpeptidation processes. The cell wall is weakened and susceptible bacteria are killed or inhibited. Resistance commonly involves replacement of D-Ala-D-Ala with D-Ala-D-Lac or D-Ala-D-Ser, reducing binding affinity. Resistance phenotype and susceptibility testing must be interpreted by organism and infection site.

## Formulation-specific pharmacology
**Intravenous vancomycin** is used for systemic susceptible Gram-positive infections and requires exposure-guided monitoring, particularly for serious MRSA infections. Renal elimination is dominant; renal function and exposure are important to efficacy and nephrotoxicity risk.

**Oral vancomycin** is poorly absorbed in most patients and produces high intraluminal intestinal concentrations. Oral capsules are indicated for *Clostridioides difficile*-associated diarrhea and enterocolitis caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* (including MRSA) according to label wording. Oral vancomycin is not a substitute for IV therapy for systemic infection. Systemic absorption can occur in patients with inflamed intestinal mucosa or renal impairment.

## Clinical evidence
Vancomycin has extensive historical clinical use and is a foundational glycopeptide for serious Gram-positive infections. For IV therapy, evidence and practice depend on infection type, organism MIC, source control, renal function and exposure monitoring. For *C. difficile* infection, oral vancomycin is an established treatment, but guideline positioning and recurrence-prevention strategies have evolved. Do not combine evidence for IV and oral formulations as if they were interchangeable.

## Safety
IV therapy can cause nephrotoxicity, infusion-related reactions (including flushing/pruritus), ototoxicity (rare and context-dependent), neutropenia, thrombocytopenia and severe hypersensitivity/skin reactions. Oral therapy can cause gastrointestinal adverse effects and, when absorbed, systemic toxicities. Therapeutic drug monitoring should follow current institutional and consensus guidance for serious MRSA infections; trough-only targets should not be presented as universal contemporary practice.

## Quality and manufacturing
Vancomycin is a fermentation-derived complex glycopeptide. Critical quality attributes include identity, potency, related glycopeptide components/impurities, degradation products, residual solvents, water content, sterility and endotoxin for parenteral products, particulate matter and container closure integrity. Oral capsules and IV formulations have distinct specifications.

## Regulatory status
Multiple FDA-approved oral and IV products exist. The current oral capsule label lists initial US approval in 1986; historical product approvals and formulation histories differ. The workbook's 1958 date should be retained only as a historical note pending confirmation of which original product/application it refers to. Verify product-specific indication and label.
